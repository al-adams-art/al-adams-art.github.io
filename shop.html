THE WAR AGAINST COOTER BROWN

Two armies want Cooter Brown. Cooter Brown wants them to go away.

In 1861, on the Kentucky–Tennessee line, one poor man’s refusal to enlist becomes an escalating bureaucratic catastrophe involving two governments, contradictory records, an empty funeral, and forty pounds of turnips.

A deadpan historical satire about a man who understands that when two authorities argue over who owns you, sometimes the smartest move is to let them keep arguing.

Part One / opening

Before the war, nobody particularly wanted Cooter Brown.

This arrangement suited Cooter Brown perfectly.

His father had wanted him long enough to teach him to fish, shoot, mend a fence, judge the intentions of a mule, and recognize the sound of a man approaching the place with paperwork.

After that, Cooter had largely been left alone.

He considered this civilization.

The doctrine

There were honest choices.

Coffee or whiskey.

Hunt today or tomorrow.

Fix the fence or accept that the cow had entered a period of geographical experimentation.

The doctrine contained one principle:

If two authorities disagree over who owns you, encourage the disagreement.

Kentucky / Tennessee

“You told the Kentucky tax collector this was Tennessee.”

“That was a tax question.”

“You cannot change states based on the question.”

“Then why’d God put the line so close?”

That was the first articulation of what would later, by people who liked naming things, become known as the Cooter Brown Doctrine.

Cooter himself would have objected to the word doctrine.

It sounded taxable.
